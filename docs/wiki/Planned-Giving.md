# Planned giving: the vehicles, read as instruments

**Status:** draft, proposed 2026-11-21. Synthetic cases, roles only, illustrative amounts. **Not legal or tax advice.**

## The perspective shift

The IRS publications ([rehearsal/10](../../rehearsal/10-irs-instruments.md)) are the user manual. Planned-giving vehicles are the machines the manual describes. Each one splits a single asset into two interests on a timeline: who gets paid now, and who gets what is left later. The charity can sit in either seat. Put the foundation in the front seat and family in the back, and you have a lead trust. Reverse it, and you have a remainder trust.

The trade in every vehicle is the same. The law gives a deduction or a tax-free sale in exchange for a real, measurable charitable interest. The red flags are all ways of keeping the deduction while hollowing out the charity's seat: paying it "net income" instead of a fixed amount, leaving it a token remainder, or letting an insider buy the asset out of the trust. Each red flag below became a check.

The map came from the GiftLaw Pro index. GiftLaw is a secondary source: a summary of the law, not the law. Only two of its 25 priority pages loaded (3.5.1 family lead trusts and 3.10.1 unitrust recipients); the site turned the rest away. So every check cites the Code or a regulation that was read, and GiftLaw appears only beside it. Later the operator's notes on all nine chapters reached the shelf, with an audit of them against current law; every example story in them is now a graded case ([GiftLaw stories](GiftLaw-Stories.md)).

## The three routes that matter most for this structure

1. **A family lead trust that holds the business.** The foundation takes a fixed annuity or unitrust payment for a term, and family members take the remainder. A lead trust is treated as a private foundation for self-dealing (4947(a)(2)). It escapes the excess-business-holdings tax only if the charity's lead interest is worth "not more than 60 percent" of the trust (4947(b)(3)(A)). So the choice is real: a near-"zeroed-out" lead trust (charity's share near 100 percent) makes the gift almost tax-free but cannot keep the operating company past the gift period. A lead trust held at 60 percent or less can keep it, at the cost of a larger taxable gift on the way in. Cases G03, G04, G05. *Statute read; the trade-off is our reading.*
2. **The estate administration exception.** If the operating company is left to the foundation, an insider (a family member or a family company) can still buy it from the estate without self-dealing. All five conditions in 53.4941(d)-1(b)(3) have to hold: a power of sale, probate court approval, before the estate closes, at least fair market value of the foundation's interest, and something at least as liquid in return. The business stays in family hands and the foundation gets cash. Cases G20, G21. *Regulation read.*
3. **Same-terms redemptions.** A family company that is a disqualified person may redeem the shares a foundation or a split-interest trust holds, if every holder of the class gets the same terms and the foundation gets fair market value (4941(d)(2)(F)). That is how a trust or foundation exits a family company without a forbidden sale. Cases G08, G09. *Statute read; GiftLaw 3.10.1 summarizes PLR 200720021 to the same effect.*

## Equipment and vehicle leases

A trust or the foundation that owns equipment or vehicles and rents them to an operating company is a live pattern. Two rules decide it, and both were read on 2026-11-21:

1. **Who rents.** If the lessee is a disqualified person (the founder, family, or a company they own over 35 percent of), the lease is self-dealing for the foundation (4941(d)(1)) and for a deductible split-interest trust (4947(a)(2)), at any rent. Cases G35 and G36.
2. **What is rented.** Rent from real property is excluded from unrelated business income. Rent from personal property is excluded only when it is leased with real property and is incidental, which the regulation puts at 10 percent or less of the total rent. It is never excluded when over 50 percent of the rent is for personal property or the rent depends on profits (512(b)(3); 1.512(b)-1(c)(2)). A remainder trust pays all of the unrelated income as excise tax (664(c)(2)(A)). Cases G33 and G34.

So a fleet or equipment lease belongs in an ordinary entity that pays market rent, not in the trust or the foundation. *Statute and regulation read; the structuring advice is ours.*

## Figures

Every number a check uses lives in `decision/figures.ttl` with the day it was read, its source and the section it comes from. `validate_figures.py` fails a figure with no as-of date, one read before a later law change to its section (PPA 2006, the 2017 tax law, the 2025 law and the latest amendment to each section the checks use), a figure with no governing section, and a threshold that no longer matches every check that uses it. A threshold match is on the whole number (50 does not match 500), and the number must also equal the figure as the law states it. `--selftest` shows it catches each of these.
