# 01. Instrument register

[Back to the rehearsal overview](README.md)

The founder's framing: read the Internal Revenue Code as a collection of instruments, each one a good question with evidence criteria the structure must answer. This register does that. Each entry says what the instrument allows, the evidence test, and who cannot use it.

The other half of the framing, from the courts: the code is read by its substance, not only its letter. Gregory v. Helvering (1935) says both things at once, and the twin keeps both:

> "The legal right of a taxpayer to decrease the amount of what otherwise would be his taxes, or altogether avoid them, by means which the law permits, cannot be doubted."
>
> "But the question for determination is whether what was done, apart from the tax motive, was the thing which the statute intended."
>
> 293 U.S. 465, 469 (1935), https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/293/465/

**How quotes were read (2026-11-20).** From Cornell LII, eCFR, irs.gov, govinfo and Justia. The reading tool limits each quote to about 120 characters, so long provisions appear as exact pieces joined by "...". Items marked **UNVERIFIED** were not read in primary text and must be checked before anyone relies on them. Design and education, not legal or tax advice.

## A. Substance doctrines (apply to every route)

### A1. Economic substance, IRC 7701(o)(1)
- **Allows:** nothing by itself. It is the test every route must pass.
- **Quote:** "such transaction shall be treated as having economic substance only if— (A) the transaction changes in a meaningful way (apart from Federal income tax effects) the taxpayer's economic position, and (B) the taxpayer has a substantial purpose (apart from Federal income tax effects) for entering into such transaction." (govinfo, 2020 edition of 26 U.S.C. 7701; no later amendment known)
- **Evidence test:** a real change in who owns, risks or controls something, and a written non-tax purpose.
- **Cost if it fails:** 20% penalty on the underpayment, 6662(a) and (b)(6); "40 percent" if undisclosed, 6662(i)(1). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6662

### A2. Sham trust, IRS Notice 97-24 and Markosian v. Commissioner, 73 T.C. 1235 (1980)
- **Quote (Notice 97-24):** "The owner of a business transfers the business to a trust" ... "The owner of the family residence transfers the residence, including its furnishings, to a trust." ... "the abusive trust arrangements may be viewed as sham transactions, and the IRS may ignore the trust and its transactions for federal tax purposes." https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/pub_n_97.24.pdf
- **Quote (the Notice describing Markosian):** "holding that the trust was a sham because the parties did not comply with the terms of the trust and the supporting documents and the relationship of the grantors to the property transferred did not differ in any material aspect after the creation of the trust".
- **Evidence test:** the trust follows its own terms; the grantor's relationship to the property really changes; an independent trustee where control matters; other beneficiaries hold real interests. (The four-factor list is commonly attributed to Markosian; the opinion itself is **UNVERIFIED** here.) The Notice also cites Schulz v. Commissioner, 686 F.2d 490 (7th Cir. 1982), the circuit that covers Illinois.
- **Why it matters here:** the stack's names match the pattern the Notice describes (a business trust plus a family trust). That is not a verdict. It means every trust in the stack has to prove substance, on paper and in its accounts.

### A3. Step transaction, Commissioner v. Court Holding Co., 324 U.S. 331 (1945)
- **Quote:** "The incidence of taxation depends upon the substance of a transaction." ... "the transaction must be viewed as a whole, and each step, from the commencement of negotiations to the consummation of the sale, is relevant." https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/324/331 (pin cite **UNVERIFIED**)
- **Evidence test:** collapse the steps. If the end result is the founder paying his own living costs with untaxed money, the route fails however many entities sit in between.

### A4. Entity classification, Treas. Reg. 301.7701-4(b) and 301.7701-3(b)(1)
- **Quote (business trusts):** they "generally are created by the beneficiaries simply as a device to carry on a profit-making business ... but which are not classified as trusts for purposes of the Internal Revenue Code." https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/301.7701-4
- **Quote (LLC default):** a domestic eligible entity is "(ii) Disregarded as an entity separate from its owner if it has a single owner." https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/301.7701-3
- **Effect:** a "trust" that runs the operating company is likely a business entity for tax, not a trust. A single-member the management company with no election is the founder himself for income tax, so it cannot pay him wages or rent from him as a separate taxpayer.

## B. Operating company instruments (Layer L1)

### B1. Business expenses, IRC 162(a)
- **Allows:** deduction of business costs, including pay.
- **Quote:** "There shall be allowed as a deduction all the ordinary and necessary expenses paid or incurred during the taxable year in carrying on any trade or business, including— (1) a reasonable allowance for salaries or other compensation for personal services actually rendered;" https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/162
- **Evidence test:** a real trade or business; the cost is ordinary and necessary to it; invoices to and payment from the entity.
- **Cannot use:** managing one's own investments alone is not a trade or business (Higgins, see E1).

### B2. Personal expenses, IRC 262(a)
- **Quote:** "Except as otherwise expressly provided in this chapter, no deduction shall be allowed for personal, living, or family expenses." https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/262
- **Effect:** the default for every personal line. The twin needs an express section to move one.

### B3. Reasonable compensation, Treas. Reg. 1.162-7
- **Quote:** "the allowance for the compensation paid may not exceed what is reasonable under all the circumstances. It is, in general, just to assume that reasonable and true compensation is only ... such amount as would ordinarily be paid for like services by like enterprises under like circumstances." https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.162-7
- **Evidence test:** a market benchmark for the role, with its source.
- **S corporation note (IRS):** "S corporations must pay reasonable compensation to a shareholder-employee in return for services that the employee provides" https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/s-corporation-compensation-and-medical-insurance-issues

### B4. Accountable plan, IRC 62(c) and Treas. Reg. 1.62-2
- **Allows:** reimbursements the employee does not report as income.
- **Quote:** an accountable plan "means an arrangement that meets the requirements of paragraphs (d) (business connection, (e) (substantiation), and (f) (returning amounts in excess of expenses) of this section." Reg. 1.62-2(c)(1). Substantiation: it "requires each business expense to be substantiated to the payor ... within a reasonable period of time." 1.62-2(e)(1). https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.62-2
- **Evidence test:** a written plan; receipts within a reasonable time; excess returned.
- **Cannot use:** a disregarded single-member LLC owner (he is not its employee).

### B5. Substantiation, IRC 274(d)
- **Quote:** no deduction for travel, gifts or listed property "unless the taxpayer substantiates by adequate records or by sufficient evidence corroborating the taxpayer's own statement (A) the amount ... (B) the time and place ... (C) the business purpose ... and (D) the business relationship" https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/274
- **Evidence test:** a log with all four, made at the time.

### B6. Working condition fringe, IRC 132(d)
- **Quote:** "any property or services provided to an employee of the employer to the extent that, if the employee paid for such property or services, such payment would be allowable as a deduction under section 162 or 167." https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/132
- **2026 note (Pub. 15-B):** AI literacy and skill programs "may be tax-free as working condition fringe benefits." https://www.irs.gov/publications/p15b

### B7. Educational assistance, IRC 127
- **Quote:** "this section shall apply only to the first $5,250 of such assistance so furnished." 127(a)(2); indexed for years beginning after 2026 (127(d), added by Pub. L. 119-21). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/127
- **Evidence test:** a written plan that does not favor owners; 127 has a limit on benefits to more-than-5% owners (not quoted here, **UNVERIFIED** detail), which matters when the founder owns the company.

### B8. Home use, IRC 280A
- **Quote (home office):** the disallowance does not apply to space "exclusively used on a regular basis—" for listed business uses. 280A(c)(1)
- **Quote (rent to employer):** the office exceptions "shall not apply to any item which is attributable to the rental of the dwelling unit (or any portion thereof) by the taxpayer to his employer". 280A(c)(6)
- **Quote (short rental):** if the unit "is actually rented for less than 15 days during the taxable year", "the income derived from such use for the taxable year shall not be included in the gross income". 280A(g) https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/280A
- **Cannot use:** renting to a disregarded entity you own is renting to yourself.

### B9. Related-entity pricing, IRC 482
- **Quote:** for businesses "owned or controlled directly or indirectly by the same interests, the Secretary may distribute, apportion, or allocate gross income, deductions, credits, or allowances" ... "clearly to reflect the income of any of such organizations" https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/482
- **Evidence test:** the management company's fee from the operating company priced and documented at arm's length.

## C. Family Trust instruments (Layer L3)

### C1. Grantor trust rules, IRC 671-679
- **671:** "there shall then be included in computing the taxable income and credits of the grantor" (fragment). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/671
- **674(a):** "The grantor shall be treated as the owner of any portion of a trust in respect of which the beneficial enjoyment of the corpus or the income therefrom is subject to a power of disposition, exercisable by the grantor or a nonadverse party, or both, without the approval or consent of any adverse party."
- **675(3):** the grantor is owner where "The grantor has directly or indirectly borrowed the corpus or income and has not completely repaid the loan".
- **677(a):** owner where income "is, or, in the discretion of the grantor or a nonadverse party, or both, may be— (1) distributed to the grantor or the grantor's spouse;"
- **679(a)(1):** "A United States person who directly or indirectly transfers property to a foreign trust ... shall be treated as the owner ... if for such year there is a United States beneficiary".
- **Effect for the twin:** grantor vs non-grantor is a switch the twin tests. If the founder can benefit from or control the Family Trust, it is a grantor trust and its income is simply his income. 679 is why the earlier foreign-trust path likely made him the owner anyway, if any trust was funded.

### C2. Distributable net income and distributions, IRC 643(a), 652(a), 661(a), 662(a)
- **643(a):** "the term "distributable net income" means ... the taxable income of the estate or trust computed with the following modifications—"
- **661(a):** "there shall be allowed as a deduction in computing the taxable income of an estate or trust ... the sum of—"
- **662(a):** "there shall be included in the gross income of a beneficiary to whom an amount specified in section 661(a) is paid, credited, or required to be distributed".
- **Effect:** a non-grantor trust can pay a beneficiary's living costs. It is lawful. It is taxed once, to the beneficiary, up to DNI. It moves who pays the tax; it does not remove it.

### C3. Trust tax rates, IRC 1 and Rev. Proc. 2025-32
- **Quote (2026 table for estates and trusts):** "Over $16,000 $3,851 plus 37% of the excess over $16,000" https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-25-32.pdf
- **Effect:** income a non-grantor trust keeps hits the top rate at $16,000. Accumulating income inside the trust is expensive; distributing it to lower-bracket beneficiaries is where the trade-off can favor Arm B.

### C4. Charitable deduction for trusts, IRC 642(c)(1)
- **Quote:** a deduction for "any amount of the gross income, without limitation, which pursuant to the terms of the governing instrument is, during the taxable year, paid for a purpose specified in section 170(c)". https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/642
- **Evidence test:** the trust instrument directs the payment, and it is actually paid that year. Set-asides (642(c)(2)) are mostly unavailable to new trusts (**UNVERIFIED** detail).
- **Watch:** once a deduction is allowed, 4947(a)(2) can apply the foundation rules to the trust (D8).

### C5. Gift into trust, IRC 2511(a) and 2503(b)
- **2511(a):** the gift tax "shall apply whether the transfer is in trust or otherwise, whether the gift is direct or indirect".
- **2503(b)(1):** excludes the first amount of gifts "(other than gifts of future interests in property)" to any person each year; for 2026 that amount is "$19,000" (Rev. Proc. 2025-32).
- **2026 basic exclusion:** "$15,000,000" (Rev. Proc. 2025-32, quoting Pub. L. 119-21 sec. 70106).
- **Effect:** funding a trust is a gift; most trust gifts are future interests and need withdrawal rights to use the annual exclusion.

## D. Foundation instruments (Layer L4)

### D1. Self-dealing, IRC 4941(a)(1) and (d)(1)
- **Quote:** "There is hereby imposed a tax on each act of self-dealing between a disqualified person and a private foundation." ... "equal to 10 percent of the amount involved" ... "paid by any disqualified person". Self-dealing includes "(A) sale or exchange, or leasing, of property", "(C) furnishing of goods, services, or facilities", "(D) payment of compensation (or payment or reimbursement of expenses)", "(E) transfer to, or use by or for the benefit of, a disqualified person" ... "of the income or assets of a private foundation". https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/4941
- **Effect:** fair price does not cure it. The foundation cannot rent the founder's building, buy from him, or pay his costs.

### D2. The one door: reasonable compensation, IRC 4941(d)(2)(E) and Treas. Reg. 53.4941(d)-3(c)(1)
- **Quote:** "the payment of compensation (and the payment or reimbursement of expenses)" ... "for personal services which are reasonable and necessary to carrying out the exempt purpose of the private foundation" ... "shall not be an act of self-dealing if the compensation (or payment or reimbursement) is not excessive". https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-53/subpart-B/section-53.4941(d)-3
- **Evidence test:** a role description tied to a program; time records; a market benchmark; approval by people other than the founder. The regulation's examples are professional services (legal, investment counseling, banking); selling goods is not personal services.
- **Effect:** this is how foundation money can lawfully reach him: as taxable wages for real work.

### D3. Who is an insider, IRC 4946(a)(1) and (d)
- **Quote:** "the term 'disqualified person' means" "(A) a substantial contributor to the foundation", "(B) a foundation manager", "(D) a member of the family", "(E) a corporation of which persons described in subparagraph (A), (B), (C), or (D) own more than 35 percent". Family "shall include only his spouse, ancestors, children" and descendants and their spouses (rest paraphrased). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/4946
- **Effect:** the founder, his family, and entities they own more than 35% of (likely the founder's companies, and the trusts by the parallel trust test) are all disqualified persons.

### D4. Payout, IRC 4942
- **Quote:** "a tax equal to 30 percent of the amount of such income remaining undistributed"; minimum investment return "is 5 percent of the excess of—" (assets not used for charity). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/4942
- **Effect:** the pipeline's engine. Assets in, about 5% a year out to programs.

### D5. Investment income tax, IRC 4940(a)
- **Quote:** "a tax equal to 1.39 percent" ... "of the net investment income of such foundation for the taxable year." https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/4940 (current through Pub. L. 119-136 on uscode.house.gov)

### D6. Business holdings, IRC 4943
- **Quote:** permitted holdings are "20 percent of the voting stock, reduced by" insiders' holdings; excess taxed "10 percent of the value of such holdings". https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/4943
- **Watch:** if the chain makes the Foundation an indirect owner of the operating company, it and its insiders together likely exceed 20%.

### D7. Jeopardy and program-related investments, IRC 4944
- **Quote (PRI exception):** an investment "the primary purpose of which is to accomplish one or more of the purposes described in section 170(c)(2)(B)" and "no significant purpose of which is the production of income or the appreciation of property". https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/4944
- **Effect:** a lawful way for foundation assets to work in the community, but never in an insider's business.

### D8. Taxable expenditures, IRC 4945(d)
- **Quote:** includes amounts "to carry on propaganda, or otherwise to attempt, to influence legislation", "as a grant to an individual for travel, study, or other similar purposes" (without approved procedures), and "for any purpose other than one specified in section 170(c)(2)(B)". https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/4945
- **Effect:** advocacy and comment work stays outside the Foundation unless it is nonpartisan education, a question for counsel.

### D9. Nonexempt trusts, IRC 4947(a)(1) and (a)(2)
- **Quote (a)(2):** for a trust "not all of the unexpired interests in which are devoted to one or more of the purposes described in section 170(c)(2)(B)," "and which has amounts in trust for which a deduction was allowed under section 170" ... the foundation rules "shall apply as if such trust were a private foundation". https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/4947
- **Effect:** naming the Foundation as the Family Trust's beneficiary does not by itself make the trust a private foundation; a charitable deduction being allowed is the trigger.

### D10. Donor limits, IRC 170(b)(1)(B), (D)
- **Quote:** gifts to a non-operating private foundation are allowed up to "(i) 30 percent of the taxpayer's contribution base" (cash) and "20 percent" for capital gain property. https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170

### D11. Annual return and recognition, Form 990-PF and IRC 508
- **Quote (IRS):** "All private foundations, whether they have taxable income for, or activity during. the year or not" ... "are required to file an annual return on Form 990-PF." https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/private-foundations/private-foundation-annual-return
- **Quote (508(a)):** an organization "shall not be treated as an organization described in section 501(c)(3)" "unless it has given notice to the Secretary"; notice is "a properly completed and executed Form 1023" "within 15 months from the end of the month in which the organization was organized" (Reg. 1.508-1(a)(2)(i)).

## E. Family wealth instruments (Layer L5)

How established families use structure, read as instruments. None of these routes pays living costs tax-free. They move **growth** out of the taxable estate, keep assets together across generations, and fund giving. Each one multiplies assets that already exist.

### E1. Family office, Lender Management v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2017-246, and Higgins v. Commissioner, 312 U.S. 212 (1941)
- **Higgins (read):** "The petitioner merely kept records and collected interest and dividends from his securities"; "No matter how large the estate or how continuous or extended the work required may be," that was not a business. https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/312/212
- **Lender (UNVERIFIED, from a secondary summary):** the office's services were "comparable to the services hedge fund managers provide"; it was paid a profits interest, and its clients were largely family members who did not own the office.
- **Evidence test:** a real business serving clients who are not its owners, paid like an outside manager, under heightened scrutiny.
- **Cost / reach:** hard to reproduce at small scale. Managing only the family's own money is Higgins.

### E2. Family LLC or partnership, IRC 2036(a), 2704(b) and Estate of Bongard, 124 T.C. 95 (2005)
- **2036(a):** the estate includes property transferred "under which he has retained for his life" "(1) the possession or enjoyment of, or the right to the income from, the property" or "(2) the right ... to designate the persons who shall possess or enjoy the property". https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/2036
- **Bongard:** the exception "is met where ... the record establishes the existence of a legitimate and significant nontax reason ... [and] the transferors received partnership interests proportionate to the value of the property transferred." (archive copy of the opinion)
- **2704(b)(1):** when the family controls the entity, "any applicable restriction shall be disregarded in determining the value of the transferred interest." https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/2704
- **Evidence test:** a real non-tax reason (central management, keeping a business together), proportionate interests, and the founder not keeping the benefit or control. Estate of Powell (148 T.C. 392, 2017) on joint powers to dissolve is **UNVERIFIED** here.
- **Fit:** this may be the cleaner modern form of what the business trust was meant to do: a family LLC that owns the operating company, owned in turn by the Family Trust.

### E3. Dynasty trust and the GST exemption, IRC 2631, 2642
- **Quote:** "every individual shall be allowed a GST exemption amount which may be allocated by such individual (or his executor) to any property with respect to which such individual is the transferor." 2631(a). "For calendar year 2026, the generation-skipping transfer exemption amount under § 2631(c) is equal to $15,000,000." (Rev. Proc. 2025-32)
- **Evidence test:** exemption allocated on a timely gift tax return so the trust's inclusion ratio is zero (2642(a)); a state that permits long-duration trusts.
- **Cost:** Form 709, trustee fees, and the trust's own returns if non-grantor.

### E4. Grantor trust sale, Rev. Rul. 85-13 and Rev. Rul. 2004-64
- **85-13 (holding as the IRS describes it in PLR 202022002):** "the owner of a grantor trust is not merely taxable on a trust's income, but is treated as the owner of the trust's assets"; the transfer "was not recognized as a sale for federal income tax purposes."
- **2004-64:** "the grantor is not treated as making a gift of the amount of the tax to the trust beneficiaries." https://www.irs.gov/irb/2004-27_IRB
- **Evidence test:** a completed gift for estate purposes while income tax stays with the grantor; no side understanding with the trustee; state creditor law checked (2004-64's own warning).
- **Fit:** the founder paying a grantor Family Trust's income tax is itself a tax-free transfer of value to the family. This is the standard reason families choose a grantor trust on purpose.

### E5. Charitable remainder trust, IRC 664
- **Quote:** a sum certain "not less than 5 percent nor more than 50 percent of the initial net fair market value" paid at least annually for a term "(not in excess of 20 years)" or for life; the remainder value must be "at least 10 percent"; the trust is "not ... subject to any tax imposed by this subtitle." https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/664
- **Fit:** a family gets an income stream; the Foundation gets what is left. Needs an appreciated asset to make sense.

### E6. Charitable lead trust, IRC 170(f)(2)(B), 2522(c)(2)(B)
- **Quote:** the charity's interest must be "in the form of a guaranteed annuity or is a fixed percentage distributed yearly of the fair market value of the property". https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/2522
- **Fit:** the Foundation gets the stream first; the family gets the remainder later, at a reduced gift value. Again, only with assets.

### E7. Donor-advised fund, IRC 4966, 4967, 170(b)(1)(G)
- **Quote:** a fund "owned and controlled by a sponsoring organization" with donor "advisory privileges" (4966(d)(2)(A)); a 125% tax where a donor or related person gets "a more than incidental benefit" (4967(a)(1)); cash gifts deductible to "60 percent of the taxpayer's contribution base" (170(b)(1)(G)(i)).
- **Trade-off vs the Foundation:** no 990-PF, no 1.39% tax, no 5% payout rule, a higher deduction limit; but no compensation door for the founder and no control beyond advice. Current text also shows a floor in 170(b)(1)(I) ("exceeds 0.5 percent of the taxpayer's contribution base"); its start year is **UNVERIFIED**.

### E8. Paying the institution directly, IRC 2503(e)
- **Quote:** "Any qualified transfer shall not be treated as a transfer of property by gift" when paid "as tuition to an educational organization" or "to any person who provides medical care". https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/2503
- **Evidence test:** paid straight to the school or provider; tuition only, not books or housing. No dollar cap.

### E9. Who can be trustee, IRC 672(c), 674(c), 2041(b)(1)(A)
- **672(c):** a "related or subordinate party" includes the grantor's spouse, parents, children, siblings, "an employee of the grantor", and employees of companies he controls; they are "presumed to be subservient to the grantor".
- **674(c):** the control rule does not apply to a power held by trustees "none of whom is the grantor, and no more than half of whom are related or subordinate parties who are subservient to the wishes of the grantor".
- **2041(b)(1)(A):** a power "limited by an ascertainable standard relating to the health, education, support, or maintenance" is not a general power of appointment.
- **Effect:** someone on the management company's payroll is not an independent trustee. A family member serving as trustee and beneficiary should be held to the health, education, support and maintenance standard.

### E10. Private operating foundation, IRC 4942(j)(3)
- **Quote:** an organization "which makes qualifying distributions ... directly for the active conduct of the activities constituting the purpose or function for which it is organized and operated equal to substantially all of the lesser of—"
- **Fit:** if the Foundation runs its own programs (workshops, curriculum, research, a museum) rather than granting, operating status may fit, with a higher donor deduction limit (170(b)(1)(A)(vii), (F)(i), read 2026-11-20). Tests and checks in F3.

## F. The founder's own ideas, tested (added 2026-11-20)

Four ideas the founder raised, each read as an instrument: what the law allows, the yes/no checks on facts, the evidence, what it costs, and who can't use it. Each red flag in the idea becomes a check, the same way the [structure checks](07-structure-integrity-checks.md) do it. Quotes read 2026-11-20.

### F1. Borrowing against appreciated assets ("lending loops")

**The idea.** Hold assets that grow, borrow against them for spending instead of selling, and let the heirs receive the assets at a basis reset at death.

- **Borrowing is not a sale (Woodsam Associates v. Commissioner, 198 F.2d 357 (2d Cir. 1952)):** "Therefore, Mrs. Wood's borrowings did not change the basis for the computation of gain or loss." ... "Realization of gain was, therefore, postponed for taxation until there was a final disposition of the property" https://m.openjurist.org/198/f2d/357
- **Basis at death, IRC 1014(a):** "the basis of property in the hands of a person acquiring the property from a decedent" ... "the fair market value of the property at the date of the decedent's death," https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1014
- **The gift-back trap, IRC 1014(e):** no reset if the property "was acquired by the decedent by gift during the 1-year period ending on the date of the decedent's death" and passes back to "the donor of such property".
- **Interest, IRC 163:** "no deduction shall be allowed under this chapter for personal interest" (163(h)(1)). Not personal: interest "properly allocable to a trade or business", "any investment interest", "any qualified residence interest" (163(h)(2)). Investment interest "shall not exceed the net investment income of the taxpayer" (163(d)(1)). The current dollar limit on home acquisition debt was **UNVERIFIED**. https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/163
- **Family loans, IRC 7872:** a loan at "a rate less than the applicable Federal rate" is a below-market loan, and the forgone interest is treated as "transferred from the lender to the borrower" and "retransferred by the borrower to the lender as interest." Small gift loans between individuals are exempt when the total "does not exceed $10,000", except loans used for "income-producing assets". https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7872
- **The Foundation stays out, IRC 4941(d)(1)(B):** self-dealing includes "lending of money or other extension of credit between a private foundation and a disqualified person;". The one exception runs the other way: a loan by a disqualified person to the foundation "if the loan is without interest or other charge" ... "and if the proceeds of the loan are used exclusively for purposes specified in section 501(c)(3);" (4941(d)(2)(B)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/4941
- **Checks (facts):**
  1. Is there a written note with a rate at or above the applicable federal rate when the lender is family or an entity in the stack, a payment schedule, and payments actually made?
  2. Is the use of the loan proceeds traced, so the interest is classed by what it paid for (business, investment, home, or personal)?
  3. Is the Foundation never a lender to, or borrower at interest from, anyone in the stack?
  4. Did no appreciated asset come back to its giver within a year of the receiver's death?
  5. Do the loans among entities have outside purposes (no circle of loans whose only effect is interest deductions)? That is the Notice 97-24 pattern of funds flowing "by way of rental agreements, fees for services," between entities, and fails structure checks S8 and S11.
- **Evidence:** loan agreements, rate set against the published applicable federal rate, statements showing payments, a use-of-proceeds record.
- **Cost:** the interest; the lender's right to call the loan or demand more collateral if the assets fall; the assets have to be held, not sold, for the basis reset to matter; estate tax still applies to an estate above the exemption.
- **Who can't:** anyone without appreciated assets: this instrument multiplies assets, it does not create them. The Foundation, in either direction except the interest-free loan to it. A loan from the Family Trust to the founder is a related-party loan: it needs all of check 1, and whether it affects the trust's grantor status is a CPA question.
- **Verdict for the twin:** a strong route once there are assets (Layer L5). No route today.

### F2. The home

**The idea.** A large family home that the structure helps carry.

- **The default, IRC 280A(a):** "no deduction otherwise allowable under this chapter shall be allowed with respect to the use of a dwelling unit" ... "which is used by the taxpayer during the taxable year as a residence." https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/280A
- **Door 1, short rental, 280A(g):** if the home "is actually rented for less than 15 days during the taxable year", then "the income derived from such use for the taxable year shall not be included in the gross income" and "no deduction otherwise allowable under this chapter because of the rental use of such dwelling unit shall be allowed". The entity that rents it deducts the rent only if it is a separate taxpayer with a real business use at fair rent (register B8, case K9).
- **Door 2, home office, 280A(c)(1):** space "which is exclusively used on a regular basis" ... "as the principal place of business for any trade or business of the taxpayer," which "includes a place of business which is used by the taxpayer for the administrative or management activities". The deduction is capped at "the gross income derived from such use" (280A(c)(5)).
- **The employee limit:** "In the case of an employee, the preceding sentence shall apply only if the exclusive use" ... "is for the convenience of his employer." (280A(c)(1)). And an employee's unreimbursed costs are miscellaneous itemized deductions: "no miscellaneous itemized deduction shall be allowed" ... "for any taxable year beginning after December 31, 2017." (IRC 67(h), made permanent by Pub. L. 119-21). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/67 So if the founder is paid wages by the management company, the home-office route is the company reimbursing him under its accountable plan (register B4), not a deduction on his own return.
- **The two doors conflict:** the home-office rules "shall not apply to any item which is attributable to the rental of the dwelling unit" ... "by the taxpayer to his employer" (280A(c)(6)). Renting the home to the company that employs him closes the home-office door for that space.
- **Estate instrument for a home, personal residence trust (IRC 2702(a)(3)(A)(ii); Treas. Reg. 25.2702-5):** a trust "all the property in which consists of a residence" ... "to be used as a personal residence by persons holding term interests". The trust must "prohibit the trust from selling or transferring the residence, directly or indirectly," ... "to the grantor, the grantor's spouse, or an entity controlled by the grantor or the grantor's spouse", and it "ceases to be a qualified personal residence trust if the residence" ... "ceases to be used or held for use as a personal residence of the term holder." https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/2702 ; https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/25.2702-5. The regulation says nothing about rent after the term ends; that, and what happens if he dies during the term, are attorney questions.
- **Checks (facts):**
  1. Is every home cost the family pays treated as personal unless one of the doors above carries it?
  2. Home office: is the space used for nothing else, regularly, for a business he runs or for an employer's convenience under a written policy?
  3. Short rental: fewer than 15 days, a real business event each day (agenda, attendees), rent matched to comparable venues, paid by an entity that is a separate taxpayer?
  4. If the home is ever placed in a trust: does structure check S5 pass (his relationship to it changed), with no depreciation or step-up (S7), and rent actually paid if the trust's terms call for it?
- **Evidence:** floor plan and photos of the office, time records, meeting minutes, venue rate quotes, rent payments, trust instrument.
- **Cost:** small. The doors carry a share of costs, not the home.
- **Who can't:** no door carries the home itself. A home bought with wages, distributions or borrowing (F1) is lawful; a home paid for by an entity as its own expense is personal (G1, S6), and a home paid for by the Foundation is self-dealing (K13).
- **Verdict for the twin:** no route yet for the home. Small routes for documented business use. The personal residence trust is an estate step for later, once there is a home to put in it.

### F3. A foundation museum

**The idea.** The Foundation runs a museum: a public program built on held assets, which is the asset-to-program pipeline in its plainest form.

- **Private operating foundation, IRC 4942(j)(3):** an organization that makes qualifying distributions directly for its own active programs "equal to substantially all of the lesser of—" "(i) its adjusted net income" ... "or" "(ii) its minimum investment return", and that meets one more test: "substantially more than half of the assets of which are devoted directly to such activities or to functionally related businesses" (assets test), or qualifying distributions "not less than two-thirds of its minimum investment return" (endowment test), or support "normally received from the general public" ... "and from 5 or more exempt organizations" (support test). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/4942
- **"Substantially all", Treas. Reg. 53.4942(b)-1(c):** "the term "substantially all" shall mean 85 percent or more." Museum assets count: amounts paid to acquire or maintain "the operating assets of a museum, public park, or historic site, are considered direct expenditures". Grants don't: the distributions must be "used by the foundation itself, rather than by or through one or more grantee organizations". https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/53.4942(b)-1
- **Donor limit, IRC 170(b)(1)(A)(vii) and (F)(i):** gifts to "a private operating foundation (as defined in section 4942(j)(3))" get the 50 percent limit (60 percent for cash under (G)), higher than a grant-making foundation's. https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170. The rule that reduces the deduction for gifts of art the charity doesn't use for its purpose (170(e)(1)(B)(i)) was **UNVERIFIED** in primary text.
- **The red flag, made a check (Rev. Rul. 74-600):** a foundation placed its paintings "in the residence of a substantial contributor, a disqualified person", where "The disqualified person exercises sole control over the public's access to his residence." Held: this "constitutes an act of self-dealing under section 4941(d)(1)(E) of the Code." ... "Although the foundation's paintings are sometimes made available for public viewing," ... "this placement in the residence of the disqualified person results in a direct use of the foundation's assets by or for the benefit of the disqualified person." https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-tege/rr74-600.pdf
- **Also self-dealing:** "furnishing of goods, services, or facilities between a private foundation and a disqualified person;" (4941(d)(1)(C)), and leasing (4941(d)(1)(A), case K8).
- **Checks (facts):**
  1. Is the collection housed somewhere the founder and family do not live or work privately, with public hours set by the Foundation, not by him?
  2. Is public access real: posted hours, visitor counts, programs, school or community use?
  3. Does the Foundation spend 85% or more of the lesser of its adjusted net income or minimum investment return directly on running the museum and its programs, not on grants?
  4. Does it meet one of the three further tests (most likely the assets test: more than half its assets are the collection and the museum's operating assets)?
  5. Does no disqualified person use the building or the collection privately: no private events for his companies, no loans of pieces to his home or offices?
  6. Is the museum's space not leased from him or his entities (K8)? A rent-free arrangement may fit an exception; that is an attorney question (**UNVERIFIED**).
- **Evidence:** the collection register (who gave each piece, when, where it is), the site lease or deed, public hours and visitor logs, the annual qualifying-distribution calculation, Form 990-PF.
- **Cost:** a building, insurance, climate control, staff, conservation; the 990-PF every year; the 1.39% tax on investment income. A museum is expensive to run well, which is why operating status needs real assets.
- **Who can't:** a foundation that only makes grants (that is the regular 5% payout route, D4). Anyone who wants to keep the art at home: a piece he keeps on his walls stays his, and a piece the Foundation owns can't hang there.
- **Verdict for the twin:** a strong long-run program route (Layer L4), and the clearest picture of "assets protected because they feed a program". It needs assets first: a collection, a site, and income to run it.

### F4. Work clothing, uniforms, and the apparel company

**The idea.** Clothing made for work as a business cost, and the family's own apparel company making the uniforms, as many companies do.

- **The test, Pevsner v. Commissioner, 628 F.2d 467 (5th Cir. 1980):** "the cost of clothing is deductible as a business expense only if: (1) the clothing is of a type specifically required as a condition of employment, (2) it is not adaptable to general usage as ordinary clothing, and (3) it is not so worn." The test is objective: "no reference is made to the individual taxpayer's lifestyle or personal taste." ... "adaptability for personal or general use depends upon what is generally accepted for ordinary street wear." The court denied a deduction for designer clothing required at work. https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/628/467/59296/
- **So suits fail.** A suit is ordinary street wear, however much the job requires it.
- **Employer-provided uniforms (IRS Chief Counsel, 2024-0009):** uniforms are not deductible by an employee "unless such uniforms are specifically required to be worn as a condition of employment" ... "and such uniforms are not suitable for everyday wear." But "the cost of work uniforms can be excludible from an employee's gross income under section 132(a)(3) as a working condition fringe benefit". https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-wd/24-0009.pdf. Since employees' own unreimbursed costs are not deductible (67(h), F2), the route is the employer buying the uniforms.
- **Logo-only clothing is not settled.** No source read says whether a polo or jacket that carries only a company logo is "adaptable to general usage". Under Pevsner's street-wear test, plain clothing with a logo may well be street wear. The twin treats it as amber, a CPA question, never a pass.
- **The apparel company as supplier, IRC 482:** sales between businesses "owned or controlled directly or indirectly by the same interests" can be reallocated "in order to prevent evasion of taxes" ... "or clearly to reflect the income of any of such organizations, trades, or businesses." https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/482
- **What the supplier line really is.** When the apparel company sells uniforms to a sister company, the apparel company has income and the buyer has a deduction (if the uniforms pass Pevsner). Inside the family the tax roughly nets out. The value is real: business volume and references for the apparel company, uniforms for the family's crews at a fair price, and a product line that can later sell to outside customers. It is a revenue line, not a personal-clothing route.
- **Checks (facts):**
  1. Is the clothing required by a written dress policy, objectively unsuited to street wear (protective gear, a costume, a uniform no one would wear off the job), and not worn off the job?
  2. Is it bought by the employer and given to workers for work, not bought by the founder for himself?
  3. Is the apparel company's price to sister companies the same as its price to outside customers (or a documented market price), with invoices, delivery records and payment (structure check S8)?
  4. Does the Foundation never buy from the apparel company or any other entity in the stack, unless counsel confirms that entity is not a disqualified person (4941(d)(1)(A), (C); 4946)?
- **Evidence:** dress policy, item photos and specifications, the apparel company's outside price list, invoices, delivery records, payments.
- **Cost:** low: the paperwork of an arm's-length sale.
- **Who can't:** the founder's own wardrobe. Clothing his company makes for him is still personal (262): free clothing from the company is pay, and a discount is a fringe-benefit question for the CPA. Suits and business attire, under Pevsner.
- **Verdict for the twin:** uniforms that pass Pevsner, bought by the employer: a route (Layer L1). The apparel company's sales to sister companies: a real revenue line at arm's length. Personal clothing: no route.
